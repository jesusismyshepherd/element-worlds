# Inside the atom (2026-11-09)

Every hall's big atom is a door. Walking up to its projector shows "Step in: the atom" (a tap on the atom
does the same); the view dives into it and you arrive in **the room inside its electron cloud**,
`/e/<Symbol>/atom`, the same room for all 118 elements, built from each one's configuration. The way
back is the arch behind you; you come out in front of the projector. A line of light on the hall's floor in
front of the projector says "Step into the atom", so the door is found.

Why (Claude's pick, 2026-11-09, when the user asked to take a part of the game and make it superb): the
atom stands in the middle of every hall, yet it was Bohr's planets, a picture from 1913. The design had
promised "later the orbitals as clouds". The real picture is the one chemistry is built on: it explains the
shape of the periodic table (the s, p, d and f blocks are the clouds' four shapes), magnetism (lone
electrons), the rule breakers (chromium, copper, gold), and it opens the way to light (electrons jumping
between clouds: the neon signs' colours), bonds (clouds overlapping: the lab's molecules) and the Atom
Forge's electrons settling into clouds.

## What is there

- **The cloud**, all round and under the glass floor: the whole atom (every subshell a glowing shell at the
  distances its electrons keep, in its block's colour), one subshell (its orbitals together, each in its
  own tint, the empty ones faint: places still free) or one orbital (the wave's two signs in orange and
  blue; where they meet, a node). Changing what is shown blends the old cloud into the new.
- **The electron map** (left of the spawn): a glass board laid out as the energy diagram, shells as rows
  (the first at the bottom), shapes as columns, Hund's boxes with spin arrows. A tap on a key's name shows
  that subshell, on a box one orbital; the key shown, tapped again, steps through its orbitals one by one
  and back to all. "All N electrons" shows the whole atom. E opens its panel: the same map (Panel.jsx's
  `orbitals` block, `src/ui/OrbitalMap.jsx`), what is shown in words, the configuration, the filling order,
  the unpaired electrons, the rule breakers and, for the whole atom, **the zoom slider** ("Shrink yourself"):
  ×1 to the depth where the 1s shell is where the outer one was (uranium about ×1000). Deep in a heavy atom
  the nucleus, drawn to scale, becomes a ball you can see.
- **The electron camera** (right): a tap takes a photo, the flash fires and one dot appears where the
  electron was found this time (Born's rule). Its panel takes 10 or 100, hides the cloud (so the dots draw
  it by themselves) and clears. The Atomling tells what the dots show at 1, 30 and 150 photos.
- **The nucleus** at the centre: a spark with a thread of light to the floor (findable from anywhere), the
  packed protons and neutrons at their true size for the view's magnification. Its panel: how big it would
  be at this magnification (in things a child knows, or in mm), the atom's size against it, its share of
  the mass.
- The Atomling (talk: what is shown, its greeting, its rule-breaking), the verse stone (Job 37:16: "Do you
  understand how the clouds float, those wonderful works of him who has perfect knowledge?", chosen for
  probability clouds: we only know chances; `scripture.md`), the arch back.
- Discoveries (`<Symbol>:atom:<find>`): each block's shape the element has (s, p, d, f), a photo, the
  nucleus, and the innermost shell reached with the zoom (elements with more than one shell).
- Shared in a room (`shared.cloud`): what is shown, the zoom, the photos' series (a seed) and count, the
  cloud hidden. Every screen draws the same photos from the seed. The Atomling's words stay each one's own.

## The science (src/chem/orbitals.js, tested in orbitals.test.js)

- Shapes: hydrogen's exact orbitals, ψ = R_nl(r)·Y_lm, with the real spherical harmonics chemists draw (px,
  dxy, dz², fz³, ...), normalised (the test integrates them).
- Sizes: Slater's rules give each subshell's effective charge (textbook values tested: C 2p 3.25, Fe 3d 6.25,
  Zn 4s 4.35). Hydrogen's shape keeps its nodes and is scaled so its outermost peak falls where Slater's
  orbital has it, n*²/Z_eff (n* = 3.7, 4.0, 4.2, 4.3 for n = 4 to 7). Slater's sizes are too big for heavy
  atoms' outer shells and for d shells (iron 3d at 76 pm against about 32 pm measured): the Scientist panel
  says the sizes are a model and quotes the measured atomic radius from `elements.json` where it gives one.
- The skin: the surface inside which the electron is found nine times in ten, the density on it found from
  200 000 places drawn by Born's rule, for charge 1 (`src/chem/orbitalSkins.js`, generated by
  `node scripts/data/orbital_skins.mjs`; the density scales as Z³ and the reach as 1/Z, so one table serves
  every element). Computed while playing it cost 16 ms an orbital (110 ms for an f subshell, a hitch).
- The filling order: Madelung's n + l rule; an element's configuration from `elements.json` (PubChem)
  against it gives the rule breakers (all 19 known ones are detected: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, Pt, Au,
  La, Ce, Gd, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm, Lr), and whether the change makes a half-full or full set.
  Superheavies' configurations are predictions (`configPredicted`), and the room says so.
- Hund's boxes: electrons spread out one to an orbital before they pair; the unpaired count (iron 4,
  chromium 6, gadolinium 8) is tested.
- Photos: the distance drawn from the radial chance r²R² (an inverse table), the direction by rejection from
  |Y|²; the test checks the mean distances against quantum mechanics' <r> = (3n² − l(l+1))/2Z.

## How it is drawn (src/worlds/atom/cloud.js)

- A ray from the eye is marched through a sphere round the nucleus, adding up each point's light: for one
  subshell the radial wave (a Laguerre polynomial, in the shader) times each orbital's harmonic (120 steps,
  48 in light mode); for the whole atom a table against log r (half floats: every phone filters them; 200
  steps, 96 in light mode, since its shells are thin and a step costs one lookup). The light at a point: a
  mist that fills the cloud and fades fast outside its skin, and a thin glowing skin (`uMist`, `uSkin`,
  `uSharp`), with a little absorption so a near lobe hides a far one (`uAbsorb`). Tune them live:
  `__ew.world.parts.cloud.uniforms.uSkin.value = 0.4`. Each ray starts at a random point of its first step
  (white noise: a regular dither showed as a mesh on the thin skins once the smaller picture was stretched).
- The whole atom is drawn as nested bubbles: each subshell a thin glowing shell where its electrons are most
  often found (its radial chance's last peak, ±12 %), in its block's colour. Shells that enclose the visitor
  fade to an eighth, so diving with the zoom you pass through a shell and see the next one ahead.
- The atom is turned 35° about the room's up (`YAW` in views.js; the march, the photos and the sparks all
  turn with it), so its x and y dumbbells are both seen at a slant from the way in; pz stays upright.
- **Sparks** (`setMotes`): 1800 faint points (700 in light mode) twinkling where the electron might be, drawn
  by Born's rule like the photos. Inside a cloud, where everything else is one colour, they give depth; in
  the whole atom they show the truth that most electrons crowd a small core (sodium: ten in a bright knot,
  one spread through the big outer shell).
- The march is drawn into a smaller picture (55 % of the pixels each way, 42 % in light mode), then laid
  over the room by a big sphere (`composite`, renderOrder −10) that the room's solid things hide and the
  bloom treats like the rest. The engine calls the world's `beforeRender()` after the camera and its view
  offset are set for the frame (a hook added for this; `engine.js` frame()).
- Two view slots (A and B) blend over 0.85 s; a change during a blend starts from whichever shows more.
- Axes: the atom's z is the room's up (pz stands upright); the atom's x and y lie on the floor, turned by `YAW`.
- Frame times on this computer: 60 fps in every view (uranium's seven 5f clouds, the zoomed atom), at a pixel
  ratio of 1 and of 2, the same as the hall. A phone (light mode) is not measured yet (`phone-check`).
- Solid things are drawn before the cloud and hide it whole (no cloud in front of the board or the
  Atomling): the cloud between the eye and a near thing is thin, and nobody has noticed.

## The room (src/worlds/atom/inside.js)

Glass floor of radius 13.5 m through the nucleus (rings every 1.5 m, spokes, a bright rim; the cloud below
shows through), a rail. The spawn at (0, 10.2) looks at the nucleus; the board at (−3.9, 8.5), the camera
at (3.7, 8.6), the verse stone at (7.4, 9.9), the arch at z 13.25, the Atomling waiting at (2.4, 10.4), beside
the way in. One subshell's skin reaches 5.4 m and the whole atom's outer shell peaks at 4.0 m (`REACH`,
`OUTER` in views.js): the visitor arrives outside the clouds and sees their shapes whole, then walks into
them.

## Traps met

- The first look was a white-out: the visitor stood inside the clouds (their skins reached 10 m, the spawn
  was at 7.6 m) and the mist was too strong, so every pixel was a flat colour. Arrive outside the clouds,
  let the mist fade fast outside the skin, keep the bloom's threshold high (0.85).
- A whole atom's density falls from the nucleus outwards without bumps: drawn as density it is fog. Even
  the radial chance (r² times the density) drawn as bands made fog once zoomed: the outer shells' spreads
  and the s clouds' inner lobes reach far in. Thin shells at the peaks read as shells; the photos and the
  sparks show the true spread. A shell all round the visitor is fog whatever its profile: it fades.
- Standing inside a cloud everything is one flat colour: the sparks give it depth.
- Text in a `why.js` entry written through a Python heredoc lost its escaped apostrophe (`Born\'s`): the
  build failed on it. Write such lines with the Edit tool.
